# Sources

Primary sources behind AIRR-1.0. Regulatory citations reflect published text as of August 2026 and are design guidance, not legal advice or a conformity assessment.

- **[NIST AI 100-1]** National Institute of Standards and Technology (2023). *Artificial Intelligence Risk Management Framework (AI RMF 1.0)*, NIST AI 100-1. Core functions GOVERN, MAP, MEASURE, MANAGE; seven trustworthiness characteristics. https://airc.nist.gov/airmf-resources/airmf/
- **[NIST AI 600-1]** National Institute of Standards and Technology (2024). *Artificial Intelligence Risk Management Framework: Generative Artificial Intelligence Profile*, NIST AI 600-1. Twelve risk categories including Confabulation and Human-AI Configuration. https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/ai/NIST.AI.600-1.pdf
- **[AI Act Art. 13]** Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 (AI Act), Article 13 — *Transparency and provision of information to deployers*. High-risk systems must be sufficiently transparent for deployers to interpret and appropriately use output. Instructions for use must cover accuracy and its metrics, robustness, circumstances affecting accuracy, and human oversight measures. Several sub-items, including performance regarding specific persons or groups, are qualified "where applicable" or "when appropriate." https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/13/
- **[AI Act Art. 14]** Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 (AI Act), Article 14 — *Human oversight*. A **provider** obligation: the system must be delivered so that assigned overseers are enabled, *as appropriate and proportionate*, to understand its capacities and limitations, remain aware of automation bias, correctly interpret output, disregard or override output, and interrupt the system. https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/14/
- **[AI Act Art. 50]** Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 (AI Act), Article 50 — *Transparency obligations for providers and deployers of certain AI systems*. Persons must be informed they are interacting with an AI system unless that is obvious, clearly and at latest at first interaction, conforming to applicable accessibility requirements. A narrow exemption applies to systems authorised by law for criminal-offence detection and prosecution. https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/50/
- **[HAX G1–G18]** Amershi, S. et al. (2019). *Guidelines for Human-AI Interaction*. CHI '19 Proceedings, ACM. Eighteen guidelines validated across 20 products, grouped as Initially, During interaction, When wrong, Over time. Maintained as the Microsoft HAX Toolkit. https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/haxtoolkit/ai-guidelines/
- **[PAIR Guidebook]** Google People + AI Research (2019, updated for generative AI). *People + AI Guidebook*. Chapters: User Needs + Defining Success, Data Collection + Evaluation, Mental Models, Explainability + Trust, Feedback + Control, Errors + Graceful Failure. https://pair.withgoogle.com/guidebook-v2/
- **[ISO/IEC 42001]** ISO/IEC 42001:2023 — *Information technology, Artificial intelligence, Management system*. Annex A controls including A.5 Assessing impacts of AI systems, A.6 AI system life cycle, A.8 Information for interested parties. https://www.iso.org/standard/42001
- **[WCAG 2.2]** W3C Web Content Accessibility Guidelines 2.2. W3C Recommendation 5 October 2023, republished with editorial updates 12 December 2024. Level AA is the level referenced by EN 301 549, Section 508, and the European Accessibility Act. https://www.w3.org/WAI/standards-guidelines/wcag/
- **[W3C AI-A11y]** W3C Accessible Platform Architectures WG. *Accessibility of machine learning and generative AI* — **Editor's Draft, work in progress.** Documents that automated alternative text delivers only "a basic and minimised understanding of an image," and that automatic speech recognition captioning is "still not adequately accessible as a standalone feature," recommending it as a complementary tool alongside curated content. Cited here for those specific findings only; the draft explicitly states it should not be cited as other than a work in progress. https://w3c.github.io/ai-accessibility/
- **[Parasuraman 1997]** Parasuraman, R. & Riley, V. (1997). *Humans and Automation: Use, Misuse, Disuse, Abuse*. Human Factors 39(2), 230–253. Foundational treatment of automation *misuse*, defined as over-reliance on automation resulting in failures of monitoring or decision biases. Note: the term "automation bias" originates with Mosier & Skitka, which this paper cites. https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1518/001872097778543886
- **[Mosier 1996]** Mosier, K. L. & Skitka, L. J. (1996). *Human Decision Makers and Automated Decision Aids: Made for Each Other?* In Parasuraman & Mouloua (eds.), Automation and Human Performance. Origin of the term "automation bias": the tendency to over-trust automated cues and under-weigh contradictory evidence. https://psycnet.apa.org/record/1996-97323-008

## Screening questions (tier source notes)

- **s1.** Does the output influence a decision about a person's employment, finances, health, housing, legal status, education, or benefits? *Mirrors the EU AI Act Annex III high-risk domains.*
- **s2.** Can the system take an action, or commit a result, without a human confirming it first? *Autonomy without confirmation raises the oversight burden under AI Act Art. 14.*
- **s3.** Does the feature process personal, sensitive, or identifiable data? *Engages privacy-enhancement as a trustworthiness characteristic (NIST AI 100-1).*
- **s4.** Will non-expert, vulnerable, or unsupported users rely on the output without a specialist intermediary? *Determines whether explanation must be legible to a lay audience.*
- **s5.** Are the effects of a wrong output slow, costly, or impossible to reverse? *Reversibility governs how much recovery design is required (PAIR, Errors + Graceful Failure).*
